## The Four Forces

In steady, level, unaccelerated flight the four forces are in balance:
- **Lift** — produced by the wings, acts roughly perpendicular to the relative wind; in level flight it opposes weight.
- **Weight** — gravity acting through the center of gravity (CG); opposes lift.
- **Thrust** — produced by the propeller; opposes drag.
- **Drag** — the rearward resistance of the airframe and the lift-production process; opposes thrust.

In **unaccelerated** flight: Lift = Weight and Thrust = Drag. When forces are unequal the airplane accelerates in the direction of the net force (PHAK).

### Lift and the wing
Lift is generated by the wing's interaction with the relative wind, described by both Bernoulli's principle (pressure differences over a cambered airfoil) and Newton's laws (the wing deflects air downward). Lift depends on **angle of attack, airspeed (dynamic pressure), wing area, and air density** — captured in the lift equation L = C_L × ½ρV² × S, where C_L (the lift coefficient) rises with angle of attack up to the critical angle.

### Drag
- **Parasite drag** (form, skin-friction, interference) increases with the square of airspeed — it dominates at high speed.
- **Induced drag** (the byproduct of producing lift; wingtip vortices) increases as airspeed decreases — it dominates at low speed/high angle of attack.
- Total drag is the sum; the bottom of the total-drag curve is the airplane's best L/D speed (relevant to best glide and maximum endurance/range).

## Angle of Attack and the Stall

**Angle of attack (AoA)** is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative wind — **not** the same as pitch attitude.

- Lift increases with AoA until the **critical (stalling) angle of attack** (commonly ~**15-20°** for typical training airfoils — *uncertainty flag: airfoil-specific; verify for the type*). Beyond it, airflow separates from the upper surface and lift decreases sharply: the wing **stalls**.
- **Key principle**: an airplane can stall at **any airspeed and any attitude** — the stall is defined by exceeding the critical AoA, not by a single speed. A high-G maneuver (steep turn, abrupt pull-up) can stall the wing well above the published 1-G stall speed.
- The published **stall speed (V_S, V_S0, V_S1)** is the 1-G stall speed for a given configuration; load factor raises the actual stall speed (see Load Factor).

### Stall recognition and recovery
Warnings: stall-warning horn/light (AoA-based), buffet, mushy/sloppy controls, high pitch attitude and low/decaying airspeed. **Recovery** (AFH): reduce AoA first (lower the nose / release back-pressure), add power as appropriate, level the wings with coordinated controls, and return to normal flight with minimum altitude loss.

### Spins
A **spin** is an aggravated stall in which one wing is more stalled than the other, producing autorotation — a descending, rotating, stalled condition. It requires a **stall plus yaw** (e.g. uncoordinated flight at the stall, such as a skidding base-to-final turn). Standard recovery is the **PARE** flow (one common mnemonic; *verify against the POH — some airplanes specify a different procedure*):
- **P**ower — idle
- **A**ilerons — neutral
- **R**udder — full opposite the direction of rotation
- **E**levator — briskly forward to break the stall
Then neutralize the rudder as rotation stops and recover from the dive. The defense is to **stay coordinated and not stall in a turn**, especially the classic **stall/spin on the base-to-final turn**.

## Three-Axis Control

A fixed-wing airplane is controlled about three axes through the CG, each with a primary control surface (AFH; PHAK):
- **Longitudinal axis (roll)** — controlled by the **ailerons** (yoke/stick left-right).
- **Lateral axis (pitch)** — controlled by the **elevator** (yoke/stick fore-aft); the elevator controls **angle of attack**.
- **Vertical axis (yaw)** — controlled by the **rudder** (pedals).

This is a fundamental difference from a powered parachute: an airplane has full, independent control about all three axes, and **coordinated** flight (ball centered) requires combining aileron and rudder. Adverse yaw (the nose yawing opposite the roll due to aileron drag) is countered with rudder.

### Stability
- **Static stability** — the initial tendency to return to equilibrium after a disturbance (positive = returns).
- **Dynamic stability** — the behavior over time (positive = oscillations dampen out).
- Longitudinal stability is set largely by CG position relative to the center of lift; a CG too far aft reduces stability and stall/spin recoverability (tie-in to Weight & Balance).

## Load Factor and Maneuvering

**Load factor** (in G) is the ratio of lift to weight. In a level turn, load factor rises with bank angle, and **stall speed increases with the square root of the load factor**:
- 30° bank ≈ 1.15 G → stall speed up ~7%.
- 45° bank ≈ 1.41 G → stall speed up ~19%.
- 60° bank ≈ 2.0 G → stall speed up ~41%.
*(Standard values; verify the exact figures for the type.)*

- **Maneuvering speed (V_A)** is the maximum speed at which abrupt, full deflection of a single control will stall the airplane before overstressing it; it **decreases with weight**. Below V_A the airplane will stall before structural limits are exceeded; above it, full control inputs or gusts can overstress the airframe (PHAK / POH).
- The **V-g (V-n) diagram** in the POH shows the safe envelope of airspeed vs. load factor.

## Ground Effect

Within about **one wingspan of the surface**, the wingtip vortices and induced downwash are reduced, lowering induced drag and temporarily increasing performance (AFH):
- On **takeoff**, the airplane may lift off in ground effect at a speed below normal climb speed and then settle/struggle to climb once it leaves ground effect — a density-altitude trap.
- On **landing**, ground effect produces a "floating" tendency in the flare; carrying excess speed lengthens the float and the landing roll.

This is a fixed-wing-specific handling consideration distinct from PPC ground handling.

## Summary

1. **Four forces**: in unaccelerated flight Lift = Weight and Thrust = Drag.
2. **AoA is king**: the wing stalls at the critical angle of attack — at *any* airspeed or attitude.
3. **Three-axis control**: ailerons (roll), elevator (pitch/AoA), rudder (yaw); fly coordinated.
4. **Spins** need a stall + yaw; stay coordinated, beware the base-to-final stall/spin; recover per the POH.
5. **Load factor** raises stall speed (60° bank ≈ 2 G ≈ +41%); respect V_A.
6. **Ground effect** within ~one wingspan reduces induced drag — a takeoff trap and a landing float.
